   his own sentences, with their places; and the famous formulas that are not his

SHELF: sayings
TIER: 1 (with tier 3 verdicts attached)
OPENED: 2026-10-25 (pass 28)

WHAT THIS SHELF IS
   Not a commonplace book, and not a second copy of the texts: it is a list of the
   sentences that get quoted AT him, each with his own wording, the place, and a
   verdict. Its use is defensive. A model holding only the poster version cannot
   tell an owner that his favourite sentence is a condensation, or that a couplet
   inside his prose was written by somebody else.

STATUS WORDS (as on shelf 3, same closed vocabulary)
   HIS              his words, found and read in the primary files, place given
   CONDENSED        his thought elsewhere, not these words
   NOT-HIS-HERE     searched in all 72 files, under two folds, and not there;
                    absence is named with the searches, and is NOT a claim that he
                    never wrote it - see the hazard below
   MISFILED         his words, but popularly put in the wrong work

THE HAZARD THAT COMES WITH EVERY NEGATIVE ON THIS SHELF
   "Not found" here means: not found with (i) the print's spelling as read, (ii) the
   long s folded to s, (iii) f folded to s for the scans that print f for long s.
   It does NOT fold spelling variants such as "ue" for "ue": a search for
   "Eigenthuemlicher Fehler der Deutschen" returns nothing, though file 33 contains
   the sentence printed with "ue". So a NOT-HIS-HERE on a famous formula is a strong
   report about the corpus, and a weak one about the world: name the fold, and let
   the reader know which files are OCR (files 27, 30-53, 60-71 are scan layers).

SCOPE OF EVERY NEGATIVE ON THIS SHELF, dated 2026-10-26 (pass 42)
   The records below say "72 files" because that is what the estate held when they were
   written; it now holds 79. The famous negatives were re-run this pass over all 79 files,
   tier 1 and tier 2 together, with a fold declared in the catalogue (long s to s, f for long
   s, ss for sharp s, ae/ue/ie resolved, single spaces kept so that multi-word phrases can be
   tested at all). They still hold: no second clause of the balanced will-dictum, no "laecherlich
   gefunden", no "ohne Gesundheit", no "Hoelle, das sind die Andern". One apparent exception was
   caught and rejected: "drei Tage" returns one hit, and the hit is in the header of file 76,
   which is the CATALOGUER'S prose, not his - a negative searched over a whole file can find the
   cataloguer's own sentence. The lessons: a fold that deletes spaces cannot test a phrase, only
   a word; and a negative over a widened estate must be re-run, not assumed.

Q-01 | HIS | "The world is my representation."
   claim: the opening sentence, quoted more often than read.
   here: the first sentence of the first book, printed inside quotation marks by
      himself - file 01 [[27]] § 1: ""Die Welt ist meine Vorstellung:" - dies ist
      die Wahrheit, welche in Beziehung auf jedes lebende und erkennende Wesen gilt;
      wiewohl der Mensch allein sie in das reflektirte abstrakte Bewusstseyn bringen
      kann" - and note what the poster drops: the sentence is true of every living
      and knowing creature, and what is unique to a human being is only being able
      to bring it to reflected, abstract consciousness. By 1891 Liepmann's
      dictionary article (file 23, S. 342) opens its summary with the sentence, so
      its status as THE formula is already ninety years old.
   answer: quote it as his, and add the half that is always cut.
   ref: file 01, file 23

Q-02 | HIS | Life is a business that does not repay its costs.
   claim: treated by many as a paraphrase of his pessimism.
   here: his own words, twice - file 02 [[279]] (Ergaenzungen zum zweiten Buch,
      ch. 19) "ein Geschaeft ist, welches die Kosten nicht deckt", and again at
      [[677]] (Ergaenzungen zum vierten Buch, ch. 46) - with his own cross-reference
      at the first place, "wie weiter unten, Kapitel 28 dargethan wird".
   answer: quotable as his, with either place; he said it twice and knew it.
   ref: file 02, G-07

Q-03 | HIS | The world is a hell, and one devil must be the other's.
   claim: the crude version goes: man is a wolf to man.
   here: the Latin tag is his, three times over - file 01 [[198]] § 27, file 02
      [[392]] § 25, and file 02 [[677]] § 60, where it turns into the stronger
      sentence: "Wer dies Letztere recht ins Auge fasst, erblickt die Welt als eine
      Hoelle, welche die des Dante dadurch uebertrifft, dass Einer der Teufel des
      Andern seyn muss; wozu denn freilich Einer vor dem Andern geeignet ist, vor
      Allen wohl ein Erzteufel, in Gestalt eines Eroberers auftretend".
   answer: the wolf tag is commonplaces he wields; the hell worse than Dante's, with
      the conqueror as arch-devil, is his own invention - and it is the honest answer
      to anybody quoting "hell is other people" at him, which is not his (G-09).
   ref: files 01, 02, G-09

Q-04 | HIS | The porcupines: cold, and needing each other, and hurting each other.
   claim: told as the hedgehogs, and as a joke about politeness.
   here: his animals are Stachelschweine, Parerga II § 396, S. 524-525 - file 05 (a
      clean transcription) and file 51 (OCR of another library's copy of the same
      1851 print); the moral is that a certain warmth is owed, and that the
      prudent distance is what good manners find.
   answer: say porcupines, cite § 396 naming the 1851 print, and quote the moral
      rather than the anecdote.
   ref: files 05, 51, spot-check 24

Q-05 | HIS | Music is not a copy of the Ideas but a copy of the will itself.
   claim: popularised as "music is the most direct expression of the will" - or
      worse, as Nietzsche's "without music life would be an error".
   here: file 01 [[324]] § 52: "Die Musik ist naemlich eine so unmittelbare
      Objektivation und Abbild des ganzen Willens, wie die Welt selbst es ist" and
      "Die Musik ist also keineswegs, gleich den andern Kuensten, das Abbild der
      Ideen, sondern Abbild des Willens selbst"; and at [[329]] § 52 music is "eine
      im hoechsten Grad allgemeine Sprache".
   answer: his claim is stronger and stranger than the poster's: music is not a
      picture of the world's particulars at all, but of the will, so its effect
      "is so very much mightier and more piercing" - the word "immediate" is his,
      and so is the comparison with the world itself, not with the other arts alone.
   ref: file 01 § 52

Q-06 | MISFILED | Talent hits what no one else can hit; genius what no one can see.
   claim: filed to "Parerga" in most quotation books.
   here: file 02 [[462]], Drittes Buch, ch. 31 "Vom Genie": the marksman simile,
      with the sentence the poster loses - that others cannot even see the
      genius's target, so they learn of it "nur mittelbar, also spaet", and "auf Treu
      und Glauben". A second and different passage exists in Parerga (file 62, 1877
      section 61 = 1851 section 60) where talent works for money and fame.
   answer: name work, book and chapter; never merge the two passages.
   ref: files 02, 62, T3-05, G-06

Q-07 | HIS | Death is the result and the resume of life.
   claim: the summary "life has no purpose" is treated as his slogan.
   here: file 02 [[748]] § 68: "Der Tod ist das Ergebniss, le Resume des Lebens,
      oder die zusammengezogene Summe, welche die gesammte Belehrung, die das Leben
      vereinzelt und stueckweise gab, mit Einem Male ausspricht" - and the lesson it
      speaks is that the whole striving is in vain.
   answer: his sentence is about death as a teacher's final summary; the flat slogan
      "life has no purpose" is a condensation, and drops the pedagogical image.
   ref: file 02 § 68

Q-08 | HIS | Tat twam asi: each self, seen from within, is everything in everything.
   claim: quoted as his motto for compassion, usually without a place.
   here: file 01 [[443]] § 63 and [[280]] § 45, and file 02 [[706]] § 55 where the
      formula is spelled out: "jedes, auch das unbedeutendeste Individuum, jedes Ich,
      von innen gesehn, Alles in Allem; von aussen gesehn hingegen, ist es nichts,
      oder doch so viel wie nichts".
   answer: he prints the Upanishadic formula in transliteration as a piece of his own
      argument; quote the file 02 sentence for the inside/outside contrast, which is
      what the ethical claim actually rests on.
   ref: files 01, 02

Q-09 | HIS | Reading: when we read, another thinks for us.
   claim: the popular counsel on reading, variously worded and variously attributed.
   here: file 48, section 291 (the chapter's closing pages carry it at [S. 456]):
      "Wann wir lesen, denkt ein Anderer fuer uns: wir wiederholen bloess seinen
      mentalen Process", followed by the pupil tracing the teacher's pencilled
      strokes. Carried by both English translators, though one of them breaks off
      before the chapter's end (spot-check 38).
   answer: quote the simile, cite section 291 of the 1851 print (one higher in the
      1877 collected edition), and name the translation used.
   ref: file 48, files 19, 20, spot-check 38

Q-10 | HIS | Natural Right: the Germans look in the clouds for what lies at their feet.
   claim: a passage rarely quoted at all, and then usually misdated.
   here: file 33, sections 120-133 (1851 print), opening "Ein eigenthuemlicher
      Fehler der Deutschen ist, dass sie, was vor ihren Fussen liegt, in den Wolken
      suchen", with his cross-reference to the Foundation of Morality section 17 and
      to the chief work bk. i section 62.
   answer: quote by sections, and name the printing: in the 1877 collected edition
      every paragraph number of this chapter stands one higher.
   ref: file 33, spot-check 37, HOW A PASSAGE IS CITED

Q-11 | HIS | A verse inside his prose that is not his verse.
   claim: a line about great work ripening unseen, quoted out of context as his poetry.
   here: file 36 [[S. 378]] ("Ueber Urtheil, Kritik, Beifall und Ruhm"), where he
      prints a poem - "Es erscheint nun: niemand sieht es, / Niemand hoert es im
      Geschrei; / Mit bescheid'ner Trauer zieht es / Still vorbei" - without naming
      its author; the English volume (file 17) carries the same verses AND a
      translator's note naming "Ernst Freiherr von Feuchtersleben (1806-49), an
      Austrian physician, philosopher, and poet".
   answer: do not attribute the verses to him. This is the second such case in this
      collection: the closing couplet of file 48 is likewise a quotation whose author
      no German witness names, and which both translators left in German (spot-check
      38). The rule: a verse inside his prose is somebody else's until named.
   ref: files 36, 17, 48, spot-checks 38, 39

Q-12 | NOT-HIS-HERE | "No truth ever passes unscathed: first ridiculed, then
   resisted, then taken as self-evident."
   claim: quoted as his, with the three stages numbered, in quotation books and in
      more than one history of ideas.
   here: searched in all 72 files with both folds: "laecherlich gefunden" 0, "drey
      Perioden" 0, "Arten von Schriftstellern" 0; "laecherlich" and "widersinnig"
      occur often, but never in that trichotomy. The three-stage formula is therefore
      not in this corpus as a sentence. As of 2026-10-26 it is absent from a
      third class of witness too: Gwinner's 1863 pamphlet (file 73) contains neither
      "laecherlich" nor "Perioden" at all.
   answer: cite it as a Schopenhauerian commonplace; if it must be given as his, name
      the edition and page of the edition you found it in - which is what this
      collection could not do.
   ref: hazard block above

Q-13 | NOT-HIS-HERE | The three sorts of authors: those who write without thinking,
   those who think in order to write, those who have thought and then write.
   claim: printed in anthologies as his, from the essay on literary style.
   here: "Arten von Schriftstellern" 0, "schreiben, ohne" 0, "um zu schreiben" 0 in
      all 72 files. The essay on style IS held here (file 47, 1851 print) but as a
      scan layer, so the honest verdict is NOT IN THIS TEXT-STATE rather than "not
      his": the trichotomy may stand in a passage this copy shreds.
   answer: say it is not in the 1851 copy held here; do not settle the attribution
      until the chapter has been read in another copy or edition.
   ref: file 47, hazard block above

Q-14 | CONDENSED | "Health is not everything, but without health everything is
   nothing."
   claim: quoted as his, and used as the plain-man's summary of the Aphorisms.
   here: "ohne Gesundheit" 0 and "Gesundheit ist" 0 in all 72 files, under both
      folds. His own health-talk is real but differently shaped - e.g. file 01
      [[196]] § 27 on "das behagliche Gefühl der Gesundheit" as the feeling that the
      organism keeps the physical and chemical laws down, which is a point about the
      will, not a maxim about doctor's bills.
   answer: call it a condensation of his counsel, in nineteenth-century English
      rhythm; the sentence as quoted is nowhere in these files.
   ref: files 01, 04

Q-15 | CONDENSED | "A man can do what he wills, but cannot will what he wills."
   claim: his most printed sentence, and the one the collection cannot find.
   here: not in these 72 files in that wording; the 1841 essay on the freedom of the
      will has the two halves separately, "ich kann thun was ich will" (which he
      calls empty) and "subjective hingegen fuehlt Jeder, dass er stets nur thut was
      er will", followed by the clause the anthologies drop - that the lowest natural
      being would feel the same if it could feel (file 55).
   answer: say condensation, not quotation; and quote the dropped clause, which is
      the misanthropic edge of the doctrine.
   ref: file 55, T3-11, G-05

Q-16 | NOT-HIS-HERE | "Style is the dress of thought," and "books are the fourth
   estate."
   claim: both offered as his in quotation books.
   here: "Kleide des Gedankens" 0, "vierte Stand" 0. His own style-counsel uses
      clothing language the other way about, and the fourth-estate notion for books
      belongs to the journalism of his century.
   answer: treat both as English aphorisms that wandered onto his shelf; a model
      asked to "quote Schopenhauer on style" should produce his own sentences from
      the style chapter, not these.
   ref: file 47, file 48, G-08

Q-17 | HIS | "We ourselves are the will to live: therefore we must live, well or badly."
   claim: the flat poster claim that life is meaningless, as though he merely complained.
   here: file 02 [[279]] § 118, "Wir selbst sind der Wille zum Leben: daher muessen wir
      leben, gut oder schlecht" - and it stands on the very page that carries "ein
      Geschaeft, welches die Kosten nicht deckt" (Q-02).
   answer: his position is not that the world mistreated us but that we are the thing
      that cannot stop wanting; the blame is not elsewhere, which is exactly what the
      poster version of pessimism prefers to forget.
   ref: file 02, Q-02

Q-18 | HIS | What a man IS, and what he has in himself, is the immediate source of his well-being.
   claim: "happiness depends on what we are rather than what we own", given as a summary.
   here: file 04 (Aphorismen zur Lebensweisheit, the chapter on what we are): "Was einer
      in sich ist und an sich selber hat, kurz die Persoenlichkeit und deren Wert, ist
      das alleinige Unmittelbare zu seinem Glueck und Wohlsein." Note that this witness
      is a normalised transcription and yields no printed page: cite by chapter, not by
      page, and say so.
   answer: quotable as his; the threefold division (what we are, have, and represent) is
      the chapter's own frame, so cite the chapter and not a page nobody printed.
   ref: file 04

Q-19 | HIS | Free time is what most people have no use for: it yields boredom.
   claim: the popular taunt "they made too much of solitude", and the converse claim that
      he idealised being alone.
   here: file 04, "Was nun aber wirft die freie Musse der meisten Menschen ab?"
      "Langeweile, und Dumpfheit, so oft nicht sinnliche Genuesse oder Albernheiten da
      sind, sie auszufuellen."
   answer: his argument is a conditional, not a boast: solitude profits a man in
      proportion to what he carries, and for the empty-handed it yields the gallows,
      which is the sentence the popular version loses.
   ref: file 04, G-14

Q-20 | HIS | He who writes anonymously and polemically has presumption against him.
   claim: quoted in debates about anonymous criticism as though it defended the anonymous
      writer.
   here: file 47 [S. 428] § 281 (1851 print, read from the scan layer which prints f for
      the long s): "Wer anonym schreibt und polemizirt, hat eo ipso die Praesumtion gegen
      sich, dass er das Publicum betruegen, oder ungefchaerdet Anderer Ehre antasten will."
   answer: the rule bites the writer, not the reviewer - anonymity is a standing
      disadvantage in his court; and note that this is the style chapter, whose famous
      trichotomy of authors this collection has NOT found in this copy (Q-13).
   ref: file 47, Q-13

Q-21 | HIS | Life and property made to depend on another man's metaphysical convictions.
   claim: his notorious intolerance about unbelievers, usually quoted from a paragraph
      nobody locates.
   here: file 33 [S. 222] § 133 (1851 print, read from the scan layer): "Es ist uebel
      genug, dass dadurch Leben und Eigenthum des Einen von den metaphysischen
      Ueberzeugungen des Andern abhaengig gemacht werden."
   answer: quote it with the section, and note his own indignation is at the dependence,
      not at the belief; the paragraph belongs to the chapter on right and politics, not
      to the essay on religion.
   ref: file 33, file 30

Q-22 | HIS | Fame among the descendants, and the harridan who is Fame.
   claim: his doctrine that posthumous fame is the only reliable kind.
   here: file 36 [S. 392] § 241 (1851 print, read from the scan layer): "Wer koennte einer
      solchen Metze noch begehren? wer moechte auf ihre Gunst stolz seyn? wer wird sie
      nicht versmaehen? - Hingegen ist der Ruhm bei der Nachwelt eine..." - the harridan
      is contemporary Fame, which he makes a person one would not marry.
   answer: his rhetoric is contempt for the living audience rather than confidence in the
      dead one; and the sentence is his own best answer to the appeal to posterity that he himself
      discounts at 01 [[298]] § 49 (see gossip shelf G-13).
   ref: file 36, G-13

Q-23 | HIS | Natural education and artificial education: the head stuffed with concepts before the intuitions have room.
   claim: "Schopenhauer on education", as a modern complaint about rote learning.
   here: file 45 § 372 (1851 print, read from the scan layer): "Wir koennen diesen Weg
      die naturelle Erziehung nennen; hingegen bei der kuenstlichen Erziehung wird, durch
      Vorsagen, Lehren und Lesen, der Kopf voll Begriffe gepfropft, bevor..." - the
      chapter's scheme being that concepts should follow intuitions, and that artificial
      schooling reverses the order.
   answer: quotable, with the chapter; both English translators carry it to its end
      (spot-check 39), Dircks completely, Saunders breaking off earlier.
   ref: file 45, files 15, 20, spot-check 39

Q-24 | HIS (read in part) | Man cannot look at anything without trying to explain it.
   claim: quoted lightly as "children explain everything, and so do adults".
   here: file 71 [S. 272] (1851 print), where he writes that a human being is by nature
      at pains to explain to himself in some way whatever he sees, so that children even
      hold a dialogue with the lifeless thing in front of them. This copy's OCR is
      defective on that very line - it prints "trebt" for "strieht" and garbles a word or
      two - so the sentence is read here, and should be quoted only after another copy or
      an edition has been consulted.
   answer: the thought is his and usable; a verbatim quotation from this layer is not yet
      safe, which is a different thing from being unusable.
   ref: file 71

METHOD OF THIS SHELF (added pass 29)
   The later records above were hunted FROM his sentences, not from anthology wording.
   The scoring script is work/schop/quote_hunt.js: it offers sentences that carry his
   generalising company (der Mensch, Wer, Man, Nichts, Alle) and a verb of equation or
   necessity, and the rest is reading. The earlier method - guessing his wording from a
   quotation book - missed about a third of the time, and when it missed it produced a
   FALSE absence, because the query was somebody else's Latin or English, not his German.
   Eight records were added this way in one pass, which is also the honest rate: the scan
   layers damage too many good sentences to be quoted verbatim at speed.

Q-25 | HIS | A healthy beggar is happier than a sick king.
   claim: the loose popular form is "health is better than wealth", or "a healthy beggar
      is happier than a rich man".
   here: file 04, Kapitel II (the chapter on what a man IS), in the text-state of the Ohle
      separate edition of 1913, which is the transcription this collection holds:
      "Besonders ueberwiegt die Gesundheit alle aeussern Gueter so sehr, dass wahrlich ein
      gesunder Bettler gluecklicher ist als ein kranker Koenig." Saunders renders it
      faithfully in file 13 - "a healthy beggar is happier than an ailing king" (read against
      the German in spot-check 46, 2026-10-26) - but the German is blunter: "wahrlich"
      (in very truth) and "ein kranker Koenig" (a sick king, not merely an ailing one).
   answer: HIS, and with a place: it stands in the chapter on the SUBJECTIVE side of life,
      which is where the point lives - health outweighs external goods because what a man is
      outweighs what he has. Quote it against anyone who files it as a remark on riches.
   ref: file 04, Kapitel II / file 13

Q-26 | HIS | He may be wholly himself only while he is alone; who does not love solitude does not love freedom.
   claim: the anthology wording, "A man can be himself only so long as he is alone; and if
      he does not love solitude, he will not love freedom; for it is only when he is alone
      that he is really free."
   here: file 04, Kapitel V, part C ("Unser Verhalten gegen andere betreffend"): "Ganz er
      selbst sein darf jeder nur so lange er allein ist: wer also nicht die Einsamkeit
      liebt, der liebt auch nicht die Freiheit: denn nur wann man allein ist, ist man frei."
      The English everybody quotes is Saunders' - file 14, Counsels and Maxims (1895): "A man
      can be himself only so long as he is alone..." - and it is NOT in file 13, the volume
      actually titled The Wisdom of Life. Note the one place where the two differ in thought
      and not in words: his German has "darf" - each one MAY be wholly himself only while he
      is alone - and Saunders wrote "can": a permission became an ability, which is a softer
      and more comfortable doctrine.
   answer: HIS in German, quotable with the chapter; but a citation to "The Wisdom of Life"
      is a misfiling - the English volume that carries it is Counsels and Maxims - and if the
      quotation is pressed for its meaning, say that he wrote "may", not "can".
   ref: file 04, Kap. V C / file 14 / not file 13


Q-27 | HIS | The whole of posterity is as perverse as the present age - and the "pack" that holds the chairs and the reviews will bark.
   claim: the comfortable story that he consolated himself by trusting "posterity" as a
      better judge than his contemporaries.
   here: file 76, Schemann's letters (1893), in his own letter about the colour theory to
      Goethe - "und die es eigentlich sind, was man Nachwelt nennt: denn das Ganze der
      Nachwelt ist so verkehrt als die Mitwelt" - followed at once by "Ich weiss, wie das
      Pack, welches Katheder und Literaturzeitungen inne hat, gegen mich bellen wird". The
      same volume shows the corresponding published thought (G-13): "Die Nachwelt wird
      allezeit ebenso verkehrt und stumpf sein und bleiben", which this collection verified
      at file 01, page-coordinate 298, section 49. DATES ARE WEAK HERE AND ARE SO
      MARKED: this collection has not established that a date-line in that layer heads
      THIS letter, so the letter is cited by file and place, and its year (about 1816,
      Dresden, from the quarrel with Goethe over the colour theory) is UNVERIFIED.
   answer: he did not trust posterity: he thought the two mobs the same size, and hoped
      only for the "einzelne Denkende" scattered through time and place - "die es
      eigentlich sind, was man Nachwelt nennt". Anyone quoting him as a believer in
      posthumous fame should be asked to read this sentence.
   ref: file 76 (letters) | file 01, [298] section 49 | G-13

Q-28 | HIS | What comes directly from Nature's hands - sky, earth, plants, trees, human faces - is there as it ought to be; with us only as it can just manage to be.
   claim: the popular form: "he admired Italian life and despised German civilisation".
   here: file 76, one of his Italian letters to his mother ("Mein Schatz... lag noch in
      der Niederlagstrasse"), on his second entry into Italy: "Ich fand, dass Alles was
      unmittelbar aus den Haenden der Natur kommt, Himmel, Erde, Pflanzen, Baeume, Thiere,
      Menschengesichter, hier so ist, wie es eigentlich seyn soll: bei uns nur so, wie es
      zur Noth seyn kann." The layer carries a date-line "24 Mai 1822" within seven
      thousand characters; whether it heads THIS letter this collection has NOT
      established, so the year is UNVERIFIED (1822 is consistent with a first Italian
      journey three years earlier, which the same letter recalls).
   answer: HIS, quotable, and worth noting for what it does NOT say: not a theory of
      climate or race, but a comparison of what is immediate with what is contrived -
      which is his aesthetic as much as his travel-note.
   ref: file 76 (letters), UNVERIFIED as to date

Q-29 | NOT-HIS-HERE | "Man is cause and effect of himself: as effect he has no freedom and no responsibility, but as being himself the cause he is; therefore man is a free and unfree being."
   claim: offered about as often as his own summary of the free-will essay, and printed in
      quotation books as though it were his.
   here: file 76, but the pen is a correspondent's: the writer speaks of "unsern Freund"
      and of how hard it is "auf ihn einzuwirken", and then sums the doctrine in that
      paradox. It is a competent summary and it is not his wording: his own text on the
      subject is the prize essay (files 35 to 37 in this collection), where the sentence
      everyone wants - "a man can do what he wills, but cannot will what he wills" - is
      not to be found either (G-05, CONDENSED).
   answer: keep it as evidence of how quickly disciples turned his doctrine into a
      paradox, and quote the printed essay for the doctrine itself.
   ref: file 76 | file 55 | G-05

Q-30 | HIS | A CATALOGUE FOR THE WHOLE SHELF | the naive certainty, reported and then
dissected | In the 1841 prize essay he interrogates an unbefangener Mensch about what the inner
witness actually says, and prints the answer: "ich kann thun was ich will: will ich links gehn,
so gehe ich links: will ich rechts gehn, so gehe ich rechts. Das haengt ganz allein von meinem
Willen ab: ich bin also frei." Then he takes it apart: "Diese Aussage ist allerdings vollkommen
wahr und richtig: nur redet sie keineswegs von der Abhaengigkeit oder Unabhaengigkeit des
Eintrittes des Willensakts selbst, sondern nur von den Folgen dieses Akts". The formula is
TRUE, and it is about the wrong thing: it reports that the act is followed by the arm, not that
the act itself is free.
PLACE: file 55 (the 1841 prize essay; no page markers, and this scan's capitalised line-starts

Q-31 | HIS | A CATALOGUE FOR THE WHOLE SHELF | the question about the will is a question about
the man | Closing the regress of "kannst du auch wollen was du willst?", the 1841 essay writes:
"Daher ihn fragen, ob er auch anders wollen koennte als er will, heisst ihn fragen, ob er auch
wohl ein Andrer seyn koennte als er selbst: und das weiss er nicht."
PLACE: file 55, about character 42,041 of the file; no page markers, so cite file and place, and
name the copy if a variant matters. (The print sets ss as sharps; "koennte" is "koennte" with an
umlaut in the copy.)
READING NOTE: the inquiry into a free will turns out to be an inquiry into the whole man, and the
answer is not a fine "no" but a plain "und das weiss er nicht": ignorance avowed, which is how
this essay keeps its humility while its author keeps his temper. Compare Q-13, from the same
prize-essay period, where the philosopher differs from the plain man only "durch die Uebung".

Q-32 | CONDENSED | A CATALOGUE FOR THE WHOLE SHELF | the sentence nobody can find | "Der Mensch
kann thun was er will, aber nicht wollen was er will" - the balanced aphorism repeated as his,
and NOT PRINTED ANYWHERE IN THE GERMAN TIER 1 OF THIS ESTATE, measured this pass: with long s,
f-for-s, ss, ae, ue, ie folded and all spaces and punctuation removed, no tier-1 file returns
"nicht wollen was", "kann nicht wollen", or "wollen was er will"; while "kann thun was" occurs
ELEVEN times, every one in file 55, and always as "ich kann thun was ich will". His own form is
a confession in the first person; the doctrine about "der Mensch", with its second clause,
belongs to the posterity.
WHAT THE ESSAY PRINTS INSTEAD OF THE BALANCE: a regress of questions - "kannst du auch wollen
was du willst?", then "kannst du auch wollen, was du wollen willst?", pushed "ins Unendliche
hin aufgeschoben", until nothing is left but the bare "kannst du wollen?" - and the tautology
the plain man retreats behind, "ich kann wollen was ich will: was ich will das will ich".
HONEST LIMITS OF THIS NEGATIVE: (a) the fold is declared, and rules out spelling variants but
not a lost leaf; (b) the estate holds WWV II incomplete (GAP-4) and many small pieces
catalogue-only, so the sentence may still stand in a work not held here; (c) a German audit
cannot see his English translators (rule 20). The claim is therefore: not in what we hold, and
his printed form lacks the second clause. SEE ALSO: G-05, now amended with this measurement.


Q-33 | HIS | A CATALOGUE FOR THE WHOLE SHELF | style as physiognomy | From the essay on
authorship and style, at the section the 1851 print numbers 282: "Der Stil ist die Physiognomie
des Geistes. Sie ist untrueglicher, als die des Leibes. Fremden Stil nachahmen heisst eine Maske
tragen. Waere diese auch noch so schön, so wird sie, durch das Leblose, bald insipid und
unertraeglich; so dass selbst das haesslichste lebendige Gesicht besser ist."
PLACE: file 47 ("Ueber Schriftstellerei und Stil", Parerga Bd 2, 1851 print), at the heading the
print marks "§. 282." - the one section number this file's scan renders cleanly, though the
numbers 272 to 287 are visible through the chapter and the English essays below carry none.
THE TRANSLATION HAZARD, measured this pass: Saunders renders it "Style is the physiognomy of the
mind, and a safer index to character than THE FACE" - but the German compares the physiognomy of
the mind with that OF THE BODY ("sie ist untrueglicher, als die des Leibes"). Narrowing the body
to the face loses the contrast he is drawing, and anyone quoting the English about style and
bodily expression will read a sentence he did not write. See also spot-check 40 on the same
translator and file 17, where an essay came out shorter than its original.
NOTE ON THE SHELF: the second sentence ("fremden Stil nachahmen heisst eine Maske tragen") is the
part anthologies keep; the first, which is the claim, is the one to cite.

A RULE FOR THE WHOLE SHELF
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
   A saying without a place is a rumour; a place without a printing is a trap. On
   this shelf every place names the file and, where the work exists in more than one
   printing, the printing as well - because the 1877 collected edition of Parerga
   numbers its paragraphs one higher than the 1851 first edition, and because the
   English volumes often print no paragraph numbers at all.

Q-34 | HIS | A CATALOGUE FOR THE WHOLE SHELF | truth borrows the shape of illusion in
order to get the will to move | Chapter 44 of the second volume, on the metaphysics of
sexual love, ends its long argument about why a lover imagines the beloved unique with a
rule about instinct, not about love: "Also nimmt hier, wie bei allem Instinkt, die Wahrheit

the sentence to quote when someone says he thought instinct was mere blindness: it is
purposeful, and the disguise is the point.

Q-35 | HIS | A CATALOGUE FOR THE WHOLE SHELF | an illusion wears a selfish purpose like a
