OPENED: 2026-10-25 (pass 27)
AMENDED: 2026-10-26 (pass 51) - the specimen record G-01, an invented quote used to test
the shelf, was REMOVED: the shelf holds other people's claims, not the butler's own.

WHAT A RECORD MUST DO
   Name who says it. A claim with no witness is a question the butler posed himself, and
   must be marked `who says it: posed here`, never dressed up as gossip. Inventing a
   rumour in order to knock it down is a game for one, and the shelf has no time for it.

WHY THIS SHELF EXISTS
   Tier 1 is what he printed. Tier 2 is what qualified writers made of it. This
   shelf is everything else that gets said about him: the popular picture, the
   anecdote handed down from the disciples, the quotation-book formula that is
   supposed to be his and is not, and the small factual errors that even careful
   secondary writers make. In a RAG collection it is the dangerous shelf: its
   sentences read like facts and are not authored by him at all.

HOW THE SHELF IS MARKED (owner's design, 2026-10-25)
   No line on this shelf needs a "LIE:" prefix. The shelf is the disclaimer, and
   the disclaimer is declared above once. What must NEVER be lost is the shelf on
   the hit: a search of this shelf prints GOSSIP before every result, a search of
   shelves 1-2 never prints it. So the marking lives in the header and in the
   tool, not in the prose - which keeps the records quotable as they stand.

   The intended door is a menu, which pre-contextualises the search:
      (1) his own texts                     -> tier 1, files 01-71 in texts/
      (2) authorized writers about him      -> tier 2, texts/secondary/
      (3) gossip: a pack of lies and half-truths -> this shelf, OUT/schopenhauer/gossip.txt
   A model that has chosen door 3 needs no further warning; a model that greps
   flat across everything does, and gets it from the tool.

STATUS VOCABULARY - one per record, and only these
   HIS             his printed words, found in tier 1 (kept here because gossip
                   keeps repeating it wrongly, or attributes it wrongly)
   REPORTED        from a witness or a disciple: true to tell, not his writing
   HALF-TRUE       a real kernel with a wrong detail attached
   CONDENSED       his thought, not his wording: the poster version of a passage
   MISFILED        his words, but put in the wrong book or the wrong century
   SECONDARY-ERROR a mistake in an authorized writer, caught against the primary
   UNSOURCED       circulating, and no carrier found in this collection at all
   LIE             nothing of the kind anywhere in the 72 files: invented

   tested at all). They still hold: no second clause of the balanced will-dictum, no "laecherlich
   gefunden", no "ohne Gesundheit", no "Hoelle, das sind die Andern". One apparent exception was
   caught and rejected: "drei Tage" returns one hit, and the hit is in the header of file 76,
   which is the CATALOGUER'S prose, not his - a negative searched over a whole file can find the
   cataloguer's own sentence. The lessons: a fold that deletes spaces cannot test a phrase, only
   a word; and a negative over a widened estate must be re-run, not assumed.

G-05 | CONDENSED | A man can do what he wills, but cannot will what he wills.
   claim: the summary of his ethics of the will, printed on a great many mugs.
   here: NOT in these 72 files in that wording. The 1841 prize essay has the two
      parts separately: "ich kann thun was ich will", which he calls an empty
      formula, and "subjective hingegen fuhlt Jeder, dass er stets nur thut was er
      will" - followed by the sentence anthologies drop, that even the lowest
      natural being would feel the same if it could feel (file 55).
   measured, pass 42: the negative is no longer a hunch. Over all 79 files with the fold named
      above, "nicht wollen was", "kann nicht wollen" and "wollen was er will" return ZERO; while
      "kann thun was" returns 11 hits, every one of them in the 1841 prize essay (file 55), and
      every one in the FIRST PERSON: "ich kann thun was ich will". His printed form is a
      confession about himself, not a doctrine about "der Mensch", and the second clause of the
      popular balance is the posterity's, not his.
      what the essay has instead of the balance: a REGRESS of questions - "kannst du auch wollen
      was du willst?", then "kannst du auch wollen, was du wollen willst?", "ins Unendliche hin
      aufgeschoben", until only the bare "kannst du wollen?" is left - and the tautology behind
      which the plain man shelters: "ich kann wollen was ich will: was ich will das will ich".
      limits: WWV II is held incomplete (GAP-4), several pieces are catalogue-only, and a German
      audit cannot see his English translators. See the sayings shelf Q-30, Q-31, Q-32.
   answer: call it a condensation, not a quotation; if the pithy line is used,
      name it as a summary of the 1841 essay and add the sentence about the
      stone, the beast and the man.
   ref: T3-11, spot-check 25

G-36 | HALF-TRUE | Nobody took any notice of his theory of colours.
   who says it: posed here, not quoted: no book in this estate tells it so flatly; the record exists
      to be answered out of his own preface.
   claim: the flat version - total silence, from everyone, for decades.
   here: he says it himself, in Latin, and he counts the company precisely. In the Prooemium
      to the Theoria colorum physiologica (file 77, own-page [[p. 1]]-[[p. 2]]) he allows that
      just ONE man of note has openly declared for him: "unicus tantum vir palam suffragatus
      est, celeb. Ficinus, Professor Dresdensis", who used his theory as the foundation of his
      own account in Pierer's anatomical-physiological Lexicon under the word "Color" (1818)
      and taught it in his Optica of 1828 - and he gives the columns (127, 129, 132, 133, 135,
      136, 146) and complains that Ficinus mixes it with his own opinions and does not claim
      for him what is his ("neque principalibus in locis mea mihi vindicat"). All the rest,
      "quam multi" so many as have written on the subject in Germany since, have not even
      attacked it but kept silence, "quod de rebus futilibus, ne disceptatione quidem dignis,
      fieri solet" - as is usual about trivial matters not worth a dispute. His gloss on that
      silence is worth the Latin: "adversus merita silentium vituperatione saepe efficacius".

      will NOT say: that the silence proves approval ("non ea mea est humilitas, ut istorum
      silentium pro judicio habeam") - he refuses to count silence as a verdict, and refuses
      to insist it came from stupidity or malice either.
